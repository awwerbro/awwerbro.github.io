Some of the 4D-STEM work I did in Missouri is getting out. 
- In this paper [published in MAM](https://doi.org/10.1093/mam/ozag079), we use Randomized Non-negative Matrix Factorization (RNMF) to achieve massive acceleration for phase mapping in 4D-STEM. Not only for crystalline materials, but also for amorphous and mixed materials. RNMF is a great drop-in replacement if you're using NMF already and feel like it's too slow on large datasets. In the final version, we also share some insights on convergence and how many components to pick.
- [in this preprint](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2607.13979) (in submission) we used superpixels to featurize large collections of diffraction patterns in an interpretable way. We start from the observation that diffraction patterns typically contain a lot of redundant information. In any in situ dataset, some regions of the detector will be consistently information-rich and have to be sampled densely, while other regions can easily be grouped without meaningful loss of information. We found that the most efficient way to group reciprocal pixels into superpixels is to group them in a bottom-up fashion (agglomerative clustering) with Ward Linkage, using the Johnson-Lindenstrauss theorem to avoid processing the entire dataset.

This work can be useful for 4D-STEM, but also for synchrotron work where datasets can easily become very large. I want to thank my co-authors Nikhila (now at Intel), Xiaoqing (now at WashU), Andrew (MU) and Matthias (MU) for their contributions, and the editors and reviewers for the truly insightful questions and comments. Both papers changed a lot across revisions, and I believe for the better.
